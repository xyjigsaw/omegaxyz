On November 20, mathematician Levent Alpöge announced in four pages that he and Claude had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis. The argument offered for inspection was not in those four pages. It was in an accompanying library of roughly 17 million lines of Lean code. The following day, the Lean and Mathlib teams reported that the proof checked and that the statement it proved was indeed the Riemann hypothesis. Explaining how the argument worked, Alpöge wrote in [his announcement](https://alpo.ge/remark.pdf), was a task still ahead of him.

Over the next three days, Anthropic and OpenAI released further work on algebraic complexity and matrix multiplication. Taken together, the releases through November 27, 2026 suggest a more useful question than how many famous problems AI has solved. Finding a proof, checking it and understanding it are distinct jobs. They need not be done by the same people, at the same pace or at the same cost.

## Riemann: a day to check, longer to understand

The Riemann hypothesis says that every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function lies on the line with real part one-half. Checking another trillion zeros could not exclude an exception farther along. What Alpöge and Claude submitted was not a longer list of zeros but a deduction intended to cover all of them.

The [joint statement from Lean and Mathlib](https://leanprover-community.github.io/blog/posts/riemann-hypothesis/) explains why the check carries weight. The reviewers isolated the existing formulation of the hypothesis in Mathlib, already scrutinized by number theorists and Lean experts. Two tools compared it with the statement the submitted library actually proved. Several checkers then verified the exported proof. The distinction matters: an impeccable deduction from the wrong specification would leave the original question untouched.

Alpöge's account also gives a glimpse of how the route was found. He describes starting from a simpler case and identifying a setting in which its ideas might extend. Claude suggested how to chain mathematical constructions together there. Rather than pooling votes on a likely answer, the collaborators were trying a line of reasoning that neither had in hand at the outset. Formal verification let outsiders check where it ended before anyone had to explain every turn along the way.

## What 88 hours bought in fluid dynamics

The Navier–Stokes work comes with an unusually detailed account of the inputs. In its [September 8 release](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/), OpenAI put the group that found its argument at roughly 10,000 concurrent agents. About 88 hours elapsed between the launch of the first agents and the result; formalization and checking took another 17 hours. The Navier–Stokes effort alone used approximately 130 billion output tokens. These are elapsed time and output volume for a parallel effort, not single-machine hours or a dollar cost.

The claim was specific: a three-dimensional incompressible fluid, initially at rest and subject to a smooth external force, can develop a singularity in finite time. It was neither a prediction about every flow nor a result for the same equations without forcing. The team assigned groups to different formulations, then redirected resources when a promising line emerged. Extra compute paid for the option to change course as well as for more attempts.

Checking the result incurred a separate cost. In October, Alexander Bastounis and colleagues [reported discrepancies](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144) between OpenAI's Lean argument and its written proof of blow-up. That challenges the use of one as verification of the other; it does not establish a counterexample. On November 20, Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster published [a separate Navier–Stokes paper and code](https://github.com/tristanbuckmaster/navier-stokes). Their release notes say the numerical certificate used in their argument was not included in that version and the interval calculation checking it was outside the formalization. In these cases, “the Lean code is public” tells a reviewer little about what remains to be done without a closer look at the release.

## Two lower bounds, and a change in what to search for

On November 23, [Anthropic](https://github.com/anthropics/formal-math/tree/main/vp-vnp) and [OpenAI](https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main/preprints/VP-and-VNP-are-different-November-23-2026) separately released manuscripts claiming **VP ≠ VNP** over every field. The question concerns the size of arithmetic circuits that compute polynomials using operations such as addition and multiplication. Both papers argue for separation: some polynomial families in VNP cannot be computed by circuits of polynomial size. This does not settle the Boolean P versus NP problem. At release, Anthropic said that models had written both the paper and its Lean formalization and that no human mathematician had checked the proof.

The next day, OpenAI released [a preprint on matrix multiplication](https://github.com/openai/math/tree/main/preprints/The-matrix-multiplication-exponent-is-nine-fourths-November-24-2026) claiming that the exponent of square matrix multiplication is exactly $9/4$ over every field. The news was the **lower bound**. Exhibiting a cheaper algorithm can lower the known upper bound. Here the authors claim that no rearrangement of the arithmetic can achieve a smaller exponent in the model under study. The accompanying Lean result covers division-free circuits computing formal polynomial identities. The exponent concerns how arithmetic work grows with matrix size; it does not predict the running time on a particular GPU.

A shortcut can be established by exhibiting one. A trillion failed searches cannot establish that none exists. Both lower-bound claims need an argument that rules out whole families of routes, not a catalogue of unsuccessful attempts. Compute may still be useful: what the model is searching for is now a *proof that there is no faster algorithm*, rather than a faster algorithm.

## The cost of discovery

My conjecture is that in much of basic science the immediate obstacle is still the cost of an informative failed attempt, not the boundary described by [Gödel's incompleteness theorem](https://doi.org/10.2307/2269028). A consistent, effectively axiomatized theory strong enough to express elementary arithmetic contains statements it can neither prove nor disprove. The theorem does not tell a researcher that the particular question on the desk is one of them. Conversely, if a finite proof exists and candidates can be systematically enumerated and checked, a search will in principle find it. The practical case for scaling is that some such searches might finish during a researcher's working life.

On the laws themselves, I take the view that they are discovered, not invented. We invent proof languages and measuring instruments; we do not choose where a zero lies or how a fluid moves. To judge the next investment, I would give two teams the same experimental budget and give one of them more inference compute. Which team first identifies a previously unknown relationship and gets it independently confirmed? That difference in time would say more about the value of discovery than another count of pages generated.
