My conjecture is that many open questions in basic science remain open for a more ordinary reason than Gödelian incompleteness. Two sets of findings may be related, and nobody has yet thought of a way to test them together. On the laws themselves, I take a fairly strong view: they are discovered, not invented. The case for giving a language model more compute is not that it can decide the answer on nature's behalf. It is that it might shorten the distance from a conjecture to its first informative test.

## What can a failed experiment rule out?

Consider a pendulum. Suppose two formulas both fit the small swings measured so far. One predicts an unchanged period at larger amplitudes; the other predicts a longer period. Ten more measurements at small amplitudes might do little to distinguish them, even with a better clock. Pulling the pendulum farther back for one more measurement could be worth more. We can invent a clock, devise both formulas and design a better experiment. We cannot invent the time the pendulum takes to return after we let go.

Trial and error involves more than increasing the trial count. A useful question comes first: **if the result is not what I expect, which explanation could I rule out?** Before anyone enters the lab, a language model could work out what each hypothesis predicts, find the range of amplitudes where the difference is largest, and check that difference against the instrument's uncertainty. If the difference is too small to measure, dropping the proposal early is a useful failure. The measurement still has to be made, but the lab need not pay the same price for every conjecture.

## Finding a path that was there all along

Mathematics offers a cleaner test because some results can be checked without an experiment. In 2022, DeepMind's [AlphaTensor team](https://deepmind.google/blog/discovering-novel-algorithms-with-alphatensor/) showed a way to multiply a four-by-five matrix by a five-by-five matrix using 76 scalar multiplications. Computing the entries directly takes 100; the best method previously known took 80. This is a count of multiplications, not a claim that the program runs 24 percent faster on any machine. The interesting asymmetry is that the combination was hard to find but does not require trust in the model to verify: expand the expressions and compare the terms with the matrix product.

AlphaTensor used reinforcement learning; it was not a language model. The lesson for language models concerns the organization of the work. Separate the proposal from the check. A model might try a change of variables, regroup intermediate terms, or borrow a representation from another field. A proof checker or an experiment then gets a vote independent of the model. When a failure informs the next search, scaling can increase the number of avenues a researcher can seriously investigate in a fixed time. Asking the model to vote on its own explanation ten thousand more times would not add a single observation.

Under specific conditions, the claim that enough compute will find a path has a precise meaning. **Suppose candidate paths can be effectively enumerated, a correct path has finite length, and each candidate can be checked in finite time. If the search eventually considers every candidate, it will find a correct path whenever one exists.** Nothing in that statement guarantees that “eventually” falls within a researcher's lifetime. A good representation or search strategy matters because it can move an answer forward from the far side of a trillion attempts.

## Where Gödel's limit applies

[Rosser's strengthened version of Gödel's first incompleteness theorem](https://doi.org/10.2307/2269028) describes a different situation. In any consistent, effectively axiomatized formal theory strong enough to express elementary arithmetic, there is a statement such that neither it nor its negation is provable in the theory. Set a machine looking for proofs on both sides and neither search will succeed. The route isn't long; under those rules it does not exist. Stronger axioms can settle some previously independent questions, but a new theory meeting the same conditions will have independent statements of its own.

The theorem cannot tell us whether an unexplained physical effect is one measurement away from an explanation. Nor does it count the problems that remain open simply because a search is expensive. My conjecture about basic science is just that: a conjecture, not a corollary of Gödel's theorem. It does suggest a practical test. Hold the experimental budget fixed and give a model more compute. Does it propose, any sooner, an experiment on which the competing theories disagree by enough for the available instruments to tell?

That is the return on scaling I would look for. A hundred plausible conjectures need not be worth more than one. If the hundred-and-first attempt identifies the measurement that separates two explanations, the research has moved forward. The connection does not owe its existence to the model. What changes is the chance that someone, in a working lifetime, will think to look there.
