In 2022, a DeepMind research team trained a smaller language model that outscored its larger predecessor on a range of tests, using roughly the same training compute. Chinchilla had 70 billion parameters, a quarter of Gopher's 280 billion, but was trained on about four times as many tokens. [Table 6 of the paper](https://arxiv.org/html/2203.15556#S4.T6) puts Chinchilla's average five-shot accuracy across 57 MMLU tasks at 67.6%, against 60.0% for Gopher. The 7.6-point gap does not establish that smaller models are generally better. It shows how much the allocation between model size and training data can matter even when the compute budget is fixed.

The debate over scaling often joins two questions that require different kinds of evidence. How much more capability will the next investment buy? And, given enough compute, can every law ultimately be discovered? The first question calls for experiments. It is the word *every* in the second that brings the work of Gödel and Turing into the discussion.

## Can model scores reveal a compute gap?

One hypothesis about Chinese and US language models deserves testing: if algorithms and data quality are comparable, and teams convert hardware into useful computation with similar efficiency, the amount of compute available could account for much of the performance gap. Those conditions are assumptions, not facts that can be read off a leaderboard. Two scores on the same task do not reveal whether the difference arose from training data, training methods, or the inference budget allowed at evaluation.

Two curves would be more informative than a single ranking. Hold the evaluation tasks, training-data criteria and inference allowance fixed. Compare the models at the same training budget, then increase that budget in steps. A gap at equal budgets cannot be attributed to the amount spent alone. If the gap widens at higher budgets, the next question is which training method makes better use of the additional compute. Chinchilla illustrates the value of such a comparison; it was not a controlled comparison of Chinese and US models.

Nor must all additional compute go into training. On a programming task, an inference budget can pay for repeated revisions to one answer. It can also pay for several distinct solutions, tests of each, and a further attempt to correct the failures. The second use buys an opportunity to catch an error, provided the tests can distinguish a working solution from a plausible one. In his 2019 essay [*The Bitter Lesson*](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~eunsol/courses/data/bitter_lesson.pdf), Richard Sutton favored search and learning because such general methods have often found uses for newly available computing power. That is a historical observation, not a promise of a fixed return on each additional unit of compute.

## Three different outcomes of a longer search

Consider a search over all binary configurations of $n$ bits. If each candidate can be checked in finite time, exhaustive search can deliver either a valid configuration or confirmation that none exists. The difficulty is cost. Moving from $n$ to $n+10$ bits multiplies the number of candidates by 1,024. If configurations are checked one by one at the same cost per check, a thousandfold speedup does not quite offset those ten extra bits. This is an expensive computation, not an impossible one.

Proof search has a different outcome. Fix a set of rules under which proofs can be checked mechanically, then enumerate candidates in order of length. If the target statement has a finite proof under those rules, even an unimaginative search will eventually find it. But days without a result do not establish that there is no proof. A successful search produces a certificate that others can check. An unsuccessful morning produces no comparable evidence.

The halting problem makes the distinction sharper. A program still running after a trillion steps might halt on the next step or run forever. Particular cases can be easy to analyze: a loop with no exit is one. The modern halting problem, rooted in [Turing's work on computability](https://cs.stanford.edu/people/trevisan/cs172-07/turing36.pdf), concerns a universal guarantee: **no algorithm can decide correctly, in finite time, whether an arbitrary program will halt on an arbitrary input.** A faster machine lets more programs finish within an observation period. It does not supply that guarantee.

## Gödel's limit is on proof

To say that the laws of nature are discovered rather than invented is to take a philosophical position. Its mathematical counterpart is close to Platonism: there would be no largest prime even if nobody studied primes. Gödel himself favored mathematical realism. In [his essay on Cantor's continuum problem](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/philosophy-of-mathematics/what-is-cantors-continuum-problem/D60596C86B5B9B99014D3CB54DF45ABD), he distinguished a question's having a determinate answer from the ability of existing axioms to settle it.

Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, in [Rosser's strengthened formulation](https://doi.org/10.2307/2269028), establishes a precise limit. Every consistent, effectively axiomatized theory strong enough to express elementary arithmetic has an arithmetic statement such that neither it nor its negation is provable in that theory. An AI can search for proofs on both sides. Additional hardware will increase the number of candidates checked, but it cannot produce a proof that does not exist under those rules. A new axiom can settle a previously independent statement; the case for accepting that axiom still has to be made. If the extended theory meets the same conditions, it too will have independent statements.

The natural sciences face another constraint: observation. Two models of a pendulum may fit the same small-angle measurements but predict different periods when the pendulum is released from a larger angle. Repeatedly fitting the old data is no substitute for pulling the pendulum higher and timing it. The most useful contribution of compute in that case may be to identify the experiment worth running, not to dispense with it.

Gödel's theorem neither predicts the return on the next training run nor supplies a reason to cancel it. Conversely, a better benchmark score is not evidence that computation can discover every law. A proposed increase in compute can be assessed on more immediate grounds: which previously unsolved tasks does it bring within reach, what independent evidence checks the answers, and at what cost? None of those questions needs to wait for a mathematical limit to be reached.
